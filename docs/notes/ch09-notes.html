Chapter 9 — Transformations

Transformations map points in the plane to new points. The three rigid motions — translation, reflection, rotation — preserve shape and size. The fourth main transformation, dilation, preserves shape but scales size. Compositions chain them together.

1 Vocabulary

Transformation
A function that maps each point of the plane to exactly one image point.
Preimage / Image
Original figure / transformed figure. Use prime notation: P → P'.
Isometry (rigid motion)
A transformation that preserves distance (and therefore size and shape). Translation, reflection, rotation, glide reflection.
Orientation
The order of vertices going around a figure (CW vs CCW). Direct isometries preserve orientation; indirect isometries reverse it.
Composition
Performing one transformation followed by another.
Invariant point
A point unchanged by a transformation (a fixed point).

2 Translations

Definition
Translation
A rigid motion that slides every point of a figure the same distance in the same direction.
(x, y) → (x + a, y + b)

The vector ⟨a, b⟩ describes the translation. No fixed points (unless the translation is by the zero vector).

3 Reflections

Definition
Reflection
A rigid motion that maps each point to its mirror image across a line (the line of reflection). The line of reflection is the perpendicular bisector of every segment joining a preimage to its image.
Line of reflectionRule
x-axis(x, y) → (x, −y)
y-axis(x, y) → (−x, y)
line y = x(x, y) → (y, x)
line y = −x(x, y) → (−y, −x)
horizontal line y = k(x, y) → (x, 2k − y)
vertical line x = h(x, y) → (2h − x, y)
A reflection is an indirect isometry — it reverses orientation (CW ↔ CCW).

4 Rotations

Definition
Rotation
A rigid motion that turns every point of a figure through the same angle about a fixed point (the center of rotation). Positive angles rotate counterclockwise (CCW).
Angle (CCW about origin)Rule
90°(x, y) → (−y, x)
180°(x, y) → (−x, −y)
270° (or −90°)(x, y) → (y, −x)
360°(x, y) → (x, y) (identity)
A 180° rotation about the origin is also a point reflection through the origin.

5 Dilations

Definition
Dilation
A transformation defined by a center C and a non-zero scale factor k. Each point P maps to P' on ray CP with CP' = |k|·CP.
Center origin: (x, y) → (k·x, k·y)
  • |k| > 1 ⇒ enlargement.
  • 0 < |k| < 1 ⇒ reduction.
  • k < 0 ⇒ image is on the opposite side of the center (rotated 180°).

Dilations preserve angle measures, ratios, and parallel lines, but not distances (so not isometries).

6 Compositions & Glide Reflections

To compose, apply the first transformation, then the second to its image.

Theorem 9-1
Two Reflections = Translation or Rotation
A composition of two reflections is either:
  • A translation (if the two lines are parallel) — distance = 2·(distance between lines).
  • A rotation (if the lines intersect) — center is the intersection, angle = 2·(angle between lines).
Definition
Glide Reflection
The composition of a reflection across a line and a translation in the direction of that line. The order doesn't matter.
There are exactly four rigid motions in the plane: translation, rotation, reflection, glide reflection.

7 Symmetry

Line symmetry (reflectional)
A figure has line symmetry if it can be reflected over a line and map to itself. The line is a line of symmetry.
Rotational symmetry
A figure has rotational symmetry if it maps to itself under a rotation of less than 360°. The smallest such angle is the angle of symmetry; order = 360° / angle.
Point symmetry
A figure has point symmetry if it maps to itself under a 180° rotation about some center (a special case of rotational symmetry).

8 Tessellations

A tessellation is a pattern of polygons that covers a plane without gaps or overlaps. A polygon tessellates if multiples of its interior angle measure sum to 360° at each vertex.

  • Regular tessellations use only one regular polygon: triangles (60° × 6), squares (90° × 4), hexagons (120° × 3).
  • Semi-regular tessellations use two or more regular polygons with the same arrangement at each vertex.

9 Worked Examples

Example 1 · Translation
Translate △ABC with vertices A(2,3), B(4,−1), C(−1,5) by the vector ⟨3, −2⟩.
  1. Add 3 to x, subtract 2 from y for each vertex.
  2. A' = (5, 1), B' = (7, −3), C' = (2, 3).
A'(5,1) · B'(7,−3) · C'(2,3)
Example 2 · Reflection across y = x
Reflect P(−2, 5) across the line y = x.
  1. Swap x and y: P' = (5, −2).
P' = (5, −2)
Example 3 · Rotation 90° CCW
Rotate Q(3, −4) by 90° CCW about the origin.
  1. Rule: (x, y) → (−y, x).
  2. Q' = (4, 3).
Q' = (4, 3)
Example 4 · Dilation
Dilate point R(6, −4) by scale factor k = ½ about the origin.
  1. Multiply each coordinate by ½: R' = (3, −2).
R' = (3, −2)
Example 5 · Composition
Reflect (2, 3) across the x-axis, then translate by ⟨1, 4⟩.
  1. Reflect: (2, 3) → (2, −3).
  2. Translate: (2, −3) → (3, 1).
(3, 1)

10 Common Pitfalls

  • Reflections reverse orientation; rotations and translations preserve it.
  • Rotate counterclockwise by default unless told otherwise. A clockwise 90° rotation is the same as a CCW 270° rotation.
  • Dilation is not an isometry unless |k|=1.
  • For reflections across y = x, swap coordinates; for y = −x, swap and negate both.
  • Compositions are generally not commutative: reflect-then-translate ≠ translate-then-reflect (in some cases).
  • The scale factor of a dilation can be negative — the image is on the opposite side of the center.
