Chapter 5 — Relationships in Triangles

Every triangle hides four special points (centroid, incenter, circumcenter, orthocenter) and powerful inequalities that limit how its sides and angles relate. This chapter develops the special segments — perpendicular bisectors, angle bisectors, medians, altitudes — and the theorems that govern them.

1 Vocabulary

Perpendicular bisector
A line/segment/ray that passes through the midpoint of a segment perpendicular to it.
Angle bisector
A ray that divides an angle into two congruent angles.
Median of a triangle
A segment from a vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side.
Altitude of a triangle
A segment from a vertex perpendicular to the line containing the opposite side. May fall outside the triangle (obtuse case).
Midsegment
A segment joining the midpoints of two sides of a triangle.
Concurrent lines
Three or more lines that meet at a single point — the point of concurrency.
Equidistant
The same distance from two or more objects.

2 Perpendicular Bisector Theorems

Theorem 5-1
Perpendicular Bisector Theorem
If a point is on the perpendicular bisector of a segment, then it is equidistant from the endpoints of the segment.
Theorem 5-2
Converse — Perpendicular Bisector
If a point is equidistant from the endpoints of a segment, then it lies on the perpendicular bisector of the segment.
Together: the perpendicular bisector of AB is the locus (set) of all points equidistant from A and B.

3 Angle Bisector Theorems

Theorem 5-3
Angle Bisector Theorem
If a point is on the bisector of an angle, then it is equidistant from the two sides of the angle.
Theorem 5-4
Converse — Angle Bisector
If a point in the interior of an angle is equidistant from the sides, then it lies on the bisector.
"Distance from a point to a line" always means the perpendicular distance. Drop perpendiculars when applying these theorems.

4 Points of Concurrency in a Triangle

Theorem 5-5
Circumcenter Theorem
The perpendicular bisectors of the sides of a triangle intersect at a point equidistant from the three vertices.
Theorem 5-6
Incenter Theorem
The angle bisectors of a triangle intersect at a point equidistant from the three sides.
Theorem 5-7
Centroid Theorem (2:1 Ratio)
The medians of a triangle are concurrent at a point that is two-thirds the distance from each vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side. Equivalently, the centroid splits each median in a 2:1 ratio.
Theorem 5-8
Orthocenter Theorem
The lines containing the altitudes of a triangle are concurrent.
Mnemonic for "centers": Circumcenter = Corners equidistant (vertices). Incenter = Inside equidistant from sides. Centroid = Center of mass (medians). Orthocenter = altitudes (perpendicular drops).

5 Medians & Altitudes — More Detail

If G is the centroid of △ABC and D, E, F are the midpoints of the opposite sides:

AG = ⅔·AD, GD = ⅓·AD ⇒ AG : GD = 2 : 1

Position of orthocenter

  • Acute triangle → orthocenter inside.
  • Right triangle → orthocenter at the right-angle vertex.
  • Obtuse triangle → orthocenter outside.
A median goes to the midpoint of the opposite side. An altitude goes perpendicular to the opposite side (line). Don't confuse them — they coincide only in special triangles (e.g., isosceles vertex-to-base).

6 Midsegment of a Triangle

Theorem 5-9
Triangle Midsegment Theorem
A segment joining the midpoints of two sides of a triangle is parallel to the third side and half its length.
midsegment = ½·(third side), midsegment ∥ third side

A triangle has three midsegments; together they form a smaller triangle similar to the original, with sides ½ the length.

7 Triangle Inequalities

Theorem 5-10
Triangle Inequality Theorem
The sum of the lengths of any two sides of a triangle is greater than the length of the third side.
Practical test: For sides a, b, c: must have a+b>c, a+c>b, b+c>a. (Equivalent to: |a−b| < c < a+b.)
Theorem 5-11
Side-Angle Inequality
If one side of a triangle is longer than another, then the angle opposite the longer side is larger.
Theorem 5-12
Angle-Side Inequality (Converse of 5-11)
If one angle of a triangle is larger than another, then the side opposite the larger angle is longer.
Theorem 5-13
Exterior Angle Inequality
The measure of an exterior angle of a triangle is greater than the measure of either of its remote interior angles.

8 Hinge Theorem & Converse

Theorem 5-14
Hinge Theorem (SAS Inequality)
If two sides of one triangle are congruent to two sides of another, and the included angles are not congruent, then the triangle with the larger included angle has the longer third side.
Theorem 5-15
Converse of Hinge Theorem (SSS Inequality)
If two sides of one triangle are congruent to two sides of another, and the third sides are not congruent, then the triangle with the longer third side has the larger included angle.
Imagine a door hinge: the wider the angle, the farther apart the endpoints.

9 Indirect Proof (Proof by Contradiction)

Steps:

  1. Assume the opposite (negation) of what you want to prove.
  2. Reason using known facts until you reach a contradiction.
  3. Conclude that the original statement must be true.
Indirect proofs work well for inequalities or "not" statements that are awkward to prove directly.

10 Worked Examples

Example 1 · Triangle inequality
Can a triangle have sides 4, 6, and 11?
  1. Check: 4 + 6 = 10, which is less than 11. ✗
No — the triangle inequality fails.
Example 2 · Range of third side
Two sides of a triangle are 8 and 13. Give the range of possible lengths for the third side.
  1. |13 − 8| < x < 13 + 8 → 5 < x < 21.
5 < x < 21
Example 3 · Centroid 2:1 ratio
In △ABC, point G is the centroid. Median AM has length 18. Find AG and GM.
  1. Centroid splits median 2:1 (vertex side : midpoint side).
  2. AG = (2/3)(18) = 12, GM = (1/3)(18) = 6.
AG = 12, GM = 6
Example 4 · Midsegment
In △ABC, D and E are midpoints of AB and AC. If DE = 7, find BC.
  1. Midsegment Theorem: DE = ½·BC.
  2. BC = 2·7 = 14.
BC = 14
Example 5 · Side-angle inequality
In △PQR, ∠P = 80°, ∠Q = 60°, ∠R = 40°. Rank the sides from shortest to longest.
  1. Larger angle ⇒ longer opposite side.
  2. ∠R (40°) smallest → opposite side PQ shortest.
  3. ∠P (80°) largest → opposite side QR longest.
PQ < PR < QR

11 Common Pitfalls

  • Median ≠ altitude (unless triangle is isosceles/equilateral and you're at the vertex angle).
  • The circumcenter is equidistant from vertices; the incenter from sides. Memorize which is which.
  • The centroid is the 2:1 split with the longer piece toward the vertex. Don't reverse it.
  • Triangle inequality must hold for all three pairs — checking just one isn't enough.
  • The orthocenter can be outside the triangle. Always extend altitude lines if needed.
  • The Hinge Theorem requires two pairs of congruent sides — without that, the comparison doesn't apply.
