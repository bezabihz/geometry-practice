Chapter 13 — Volume

Volume measures the amount of 3-D space a solid occupies. Every formula reduces to base × height for prisms/cylinders, and a 1/3 factor for pyramids/cones. The sphere has its own formula: (4/3)πr³.

1 Vocabulary & Cavalieri's Principle

Volume
The amount of space a 3-D solid occupies, measured in cubic units (cm³, in³, etc.).
Right vs oblique solid
A solid is right if the axis joining the centers of the bases is perpendicular to the base; otherwise oblique.
Height (h) of a solid
The perpendicular distance between the bases (not the slant!).
Postulate / Theorem
Cavalieri's Principle
If two solids have the same height and the same cross-sectional area at every level, then they have the same volume.
Cavalieri's Principle is why an oblique prism (or cylinder, pyramid, cone) has the same volume as the corresponding right solid with the same base and height.

2 Volume of a Prism

Theorem 13-1
Volume of a Prism
The volume equals the area of the base times the height.
V = B · h

For a rectangular prism (box) with length, width, height:

V = ℓ · w · h

For a cube with edge s:

V = s³

3 Volume of a Cylinder

Theorem 13-2
Volume of a Cylinder
The volume equals the area of the circular base times the height.
V = π r² · h

4 Volume of a Pyramid

Theorem 13-3
Volume of a Pyramid
The volume equals one-third the area of the base times the height.
V = ⅓ · B · h
A pyramid is exactly one-third of a prism with the same base and height. Verify: three congruent pyramids fit inside a cube exactly.

5 Volume of a Cone

Theorem 13-4
Volume of a Cone
The volume equals one-third the area of the circular base times the height.
V = ⅓ · π r² · h

Same 1/3 factor as a pyramid — a cone is the "round pyramid."

6 Volume of a Sphere

Theorem 13-5
Volume of a Sphere
The volume of a sphere with radius r:
V = (4/3) π r³

A hemisphere has half this volume: V = (2/3)πr³.

Archimedes' insight: a sphere is exactly two-thirds the volume of the smallest cylinder that contains it (the cylinder with the same radius and height = 2r).

7 Oblique Solids

By Cavalieri's Principle, the volume formulas for right solids work for oblique solids too — as long as h is the perpendicular height between the parallel bases (or from the base to the apex), not the slant length.

Don't use the slant edge as h. The perpendicular distance from the apex (or top base) down to the base plane is the height.

8 Volumes of Similar Solids

Theorem 13-6
Ratio of Volumes
If two solids are similar with scale factor (linear ratio) a/b:
  • Ratio of perimeters / sides = a/b.
  • Ratio of surface areas = a²/b².
  • Ratio of volumes = a³/b³.
To recover the linear scale factor from a volume ratio, take the cube root: if volumes are in ratio 8:125, sides are in ratio 2:5.

9 Composite & Real-World Solids

  1. Identify the elementary solids that make up the figure.
  2. Calculate the volume of each piece.
  3. Add volumes of pieces that combine to form the solid; subtract any hollowed-out regions.

Density / capacity problems use mass = density · volume or convert between cubic units (1 m³ = 1000 L = 1 000 000 cm³).

10 Worked Examples

Example 1 · Rectangular prism
A box has dimensions 4 × 6 × 10. Volume?
  1. V = 4·6·10 = 240 cubic units.
V = 240
Example 2 · Cylinder
A cylinder has r = 5 and h = 8. Volume?
  1. V = π·25·8 = 200π ≈ 628.3.
V = 200π
Example 3 · Square pyramid
A pyramid has a square base of side 9 and height 12. Volume?
  1. B = 81.
  2. V = ⅓·81·12 = 324.
V = 324
Example 4 · Cone
A cone has r = 6 and h = 10. Volume?
  1. V = ⅓·π·36·10 = 120π ≈ 376.99.
V = 120π
Example 5 · Sphere
Sphere with diameter 12. Volume?
  1. r = 6.
  2. V = (4/3)π·6³ = (4/3)·216·π = 288π ≈ 904.78.
V = 288π
Example 6 · Volume ratio
Two similar pyramids have base sides in ratio 2:3. If the smaller has volume 16, find the larger.
  1. Volume ratio = (2/3)³ = 8/27.
  2. 16/V_large = 8/27 → V_large = 16·27/8 = 54.
V_large = 54
Example 7 · Composite
A cylinder (r = 3, h = 10) has a hemisphere of radius 3 on top. Find the total volume in terms of π.
  1. Cylinder: π·9·10 = 90π.
  2. Hemisphere: (2/3)π·27 = 18π.
  3. Total: 90π + 18π = 108π.
V = 108π

11 Common Pitfalls

  • Volume is in cubic units (cm³, in³). Don't forget the cube.
  • Use the perpendicular height, never the slant height, in volume formulas.
  • Pyramid and cone have a 1/3 factor; prism and cylinder do not.
  • The sphere formula is (4/3)πr³ — three appearances of r, not two.
  • For composite solids, watch the units and don't double-count overlapping regions.
  • Volume ratio of similar solids is the cube of the linear ratio — easy to mistake for the square.
  • If you're given diameter, halve it to get the radius before plugging into r² or r³.
