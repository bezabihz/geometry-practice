Chapter 1 — Foundations of Geometry

Build a vocabulary of undefined terms, learn the postulates that govern points/lines/planes, and master the measurement of segments and angles. Every later chapter rests on these tools.

1 Vocabulary & Undefined Terms

Three terms are undefined in Euclidean geometry — they are described, not defined, and every other definition is built from them.

Point
A specific location. Has no size (zero dimensions). Named with a capital letter (point A).
Line
Extends infinitely in two directions. One dimension. Named by two points: AB↔ or by a lowercase letter ℓ.
Plane
A flat surface extending infinitely in all directions. Two dimensions. Named with a script letter (plane M) or three non-collinear points.
Collinear points
Points that lie on the same line.
Coplanar points
Points that lie in the same plane. Any three points are coplanar; four points may or may not be.
Segment AB
The set of points A, B, and all points between them. Has two endpoints and finite length.
Ray AB →
Starts at endpoint A and extends through B infinitely. One endpoint, one direction.
Opposite rays
Two rays sharing the same endpoint that form a straight line (point together in opposite directions).
Angle
Two rays sharing a common endpoint called the vertex. Measured in degrees (0° – 360°).
Congruent
Figures that have the same size and shape. Segments are congruent when they have equal lengths; angles when they have equal measures. Symbol: ≅.
Bisector
A point, ray, line, or plane that divides a segment or angle into two congruent parts.
Equal vs Congruent. Use equal for measures (AB = 7, m∠1 = 30°) and congruent for figures (AB ≅ CD, ∠1 ≅ ∠2).

2 Postulates of Points, Lines & Planes

A postulate (or axiom) is a statement accepted without proof. These are the building blocks of every later theorem.

Postulate 1-1
Two-Point Line Postulate
Through any two distinct points there is exactly one line.
Postulate 1-2
Line Intersection Postulate
If two distinct lines intersect, they intersect in exactly one point.
Postulate 1-3
Plane Intersection Postulate
If two distinct planes intersect, their intersection is exactly one line.
Postulate 1-4
Three-Point Plane Postulate
Through any three non-collinear points there is exactly one plane.
Postulate 1-5
Ruler Postulate
The points on a line can be paired one-to-one with the real numbers. The distance between two points equals the absolute value of the difference of their coordinates: AB = |a − b|.
Postulate 1-6
Segment Addition Postulate
If B is between A and C on a line, then AB + BC = AC. Conversely, if AB + BC = AC, then B lies between A and C.
Postulate 1-7
Protractor Postulate
Given a ray OA, every other ray from O on one side of OA can be paired with a number 0–180. The measure of an angle is the positive difference of these two numbers.
Postulate 1-8
Angle Addition Postulate
If D lies in the interior of ∠ABC, then m∠ABD + m∠DBC = m∠ABC.

3 Segments, Midpoint & Bisectors

Definition
Midpoint
A point M is the midpoint of AB if it is on AB and AM = MB. Equivalently, AM = MB = ½·AB.
Theorem 1-1
Midpoint Theorem
If M is the midpoint of AB, then AM ≅ MB and each equals ½·AB.
Definition
Segment bisector
A point, line, ray, segment, or plane that intersects a segment at its midpoint, dividing it into two congruent halves.
Setting up midpoint equations: write AM = MB as an algebraic equation, solve for the variable, and substitute back. Then verify the lengths sum to AC.

5 Angle Pair Relationships & Theorems

Angle pairs (definitions)

Adjacent angles
Share a common vertex and side, but no interior points.
Vertical angles
Two non-adjacent angles formed by two intersecting lines (opposite each other at the vertex).
Linear pair
Two adjacent angles whose non-common sides form a straight line. A linear pair is always supplementary.
Complementary angles
Two angles whose measures sum to 90°.
Supplementary angles
Two angles whose measures sum to 180°.

Theorems on angle pairs

Theorem 1-2
Vertical Angles Theorem
Vertical angles are congruent.
Proof sketch: ∠1 and ∠2 form a linear pair → m∠1+m∠2=180°. ∠2 and ∠3 also form a linear pair → m∠2+m∠3=180°. Subtracting gives m∠1=m∠3.
Theorem 1-3
Congruent Supplements Theorem
If two angles are supplementary to the same angle (or to congruent angles), then they are congruent to each other.
Theorem 1-4
Congruent Complements Theorem
If two angles are complementary to the same angle (or to congruent angles), then they are congruent.
Theorem 1-5
Right Angles Theorem
All right angles are congruent.
Theorem 1-6
Perpendicular Right-Angle Theorem
If two lines are perpendicular, then they form four right angles.
Theorem 1-7
Linear Pair Postulate (sometimes a theorem)
The angles in a linear pair are supplementary: their measures sum to 180°.
Vertical angles are always congruent, but a linear pair is always supplementary. Don't confuse the two — vertical pairs are opposite, linear pairs are adjacent and form a line.

6 Coordinate Geometry — Distance & Midpoint

Distance Formula (from Pythagorean Theorem)

d = √[(x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²]

Derived by treating the segment between (x₁,y₁) and (x₂,y₂) as the hypotenuse of a right triangle whose legs are Δx and Δy.

Midpoint Formula

M = ((x₁ + x₂)/2 , (y₁ + y₂)/2)

The midpoint of a segment is the average of the endpoints' coordinates.

Number-line distance

AB = |a − b|

From the Ruler Postulate. Always non-negative — distance has no sign.

When asked to find an endpoint given a midpoint, solve (x_end) = 2(x_mid) − x_other. The midpoint formula rearranged.

7 Worked Examples

Example 1 · Segment Addition
B is between A and C. AB = 2x+1, BC = 3x−4, AC = 22. Find x and BC.
  1. By Segment Addition: AB + BC = AC → (2x+1) + (3x−4) = 22.
  2. Combine: 5x − 3 = 22 → 5x = 25 → x = 5.
  3. Substitute: BC = 3(5) − 4 = 11.
x = 5, BC = 11
Example 2 · Midpoint & Distance
Find the midpoint and length of segment PQ with P(−3, 4) and Q(5, −2).
  1. Midpoint: M = ((−3+5)/2, (4+(−2))/2) = (1, 1).
  2. Distance: d = √[(5−(−3))² + (−2−4)²] = √[64 + 36] = √100 = 10.
M = (1, 1), PQ = 10
Example 3 · Vertical Angles
Two lines intersect. One angle measures (4x + 10)°; its vertical pair measures (6x − 20)°. Find x.
  1. Vertical angles are congruent → 4x + 10 = 6x − 20.
  2. Solve: 30 = 2x → x = 15.
  3. Check: both angles = 4(15)+10 = 70° ✓.
x = 15° (each angle 70°)
Example 4 · Angle Bisector
Ray BD bisects ∠ABC. If m∠ABD = (5x − 3)° and m∠DBC = (3x + 11)°, find m∠ABC.
  1. Bisector → halves are equal: 5x − 3 = 3x + 11 → 2x = 14 → x = 7.
  2. Each half: 5(7) − 3 = 32°.
  3. Whole angle: 2 · 32 = 64°.
m∠ABC = 64°

8 Common Pitfalls

  • Drawing a picture is not a proof. Diagrams can mislead; rely on given information.
  • Watch for "B is between A and C" — only then does AB + BC = AC.
  • Don't write AB = CD when you mean AB ≅ CD (or vice versa). Equality is for measures; congruence for figures.
  • Distance is always positive. The expression under the square root in the distance formula is never negative because each term is squared.
  • Vertical ≠ "up and down." Vertical angles are across from each other at an intersection.
  • Complementary sums to 90° (C → corner); Supplementary sums to 180° (S → straight).
